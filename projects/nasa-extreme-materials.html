Wear- and Corrosion-Resistant Coatings for Spacesuit Materials

Explore Research

Through the NASA L'SPACE (Lucy Student Pipeline Accelerator and Competency Enabler) program, I led the materials-design track of a mission concept proposing extreme-environment coatings for crewed spacesuit and structural spaceship surfaces. The stakes are well documented: NASA's TP-2009-214786 Lunar Dust Effects on Spacesuit Systems report found that the three EVAs of the final Apollo mission damaged the outer fabric of the suits beyond what additional EVAs could tolerate, and the Artemis xEMU/AxEMU programs target much longer surface operations on a regolith- and atomic-oxygen-rich service envelope. The work combined CALPHAD-guided alloy and ceramic phase prediction with high-throughput tribological and microstructural characterization to nominate coating systems that survive the LEO-to-lunar envelope. As a brief summary of my research:

  • The environment we're designing against. The lunar equatorial surface swings from ~398 K (125 °C) at noon to ~96 K (-177 °C) at night -- a 300 °C peak-to-peak thermal cycle that opens >200 °C gradients across a few millimeters of shadow. Permanently shadowed regions sit near 20 K. Above LEO, NASA's MISSE PEACE Polymers experiment measured atomic-oxygen erosion yields of 41 polymers on the ISS exterior; Kapton H -- the spacecraft benchmark -- erodes at 3 × 10-24 cm3/atom. The coating envelope we shortlisted had to survive both regimes.
  • CALPHAD-guided alloy and ceramic design. I screened candidate chemistries with Thermo-Calc and Pandat CALPHAD databases for phase equilibria, transformation pathways, and sintering behavior across the LEO-to-lunar temperature window. The pass shortlisted ~40 candidate systems from a several-hundred initial pool, biased toward phases that stay structurally stable under repeated thermal cycling and resist atomic-oxygen-driven oxidation. The reference point for "what the field already has" was NASA Glenn's GRX-810 -- a Co-Cr-Ni medium-entropy ODS alloy that, in NASA's own characterization, delivers ~2x strength at 1,100 °C and ~1,000x longer creep life than the prior nickel-superalloy baseline, plus 2x oxidation resistance and 4x ductility, and was licensed to Linde AMT in 2024.
  • Nanoindentation and tribological characterization. I built a high-throughput nanoindentation protocol resolving hardness, elastic modulus, and wear rate across both thin-film and bulk samples in a single sample-mounting pass, cutting characterization wall-clock by roughly 3x. Coupled to pin-on-disc tribometry, the protocol returned per-candidate Archard wear coefficients and adhesion-failure thresholds that fed back into the CALPHAD shortlist. The acceptance bar borrowed from the NASA EVA Technology Development Program's 2008 abrasion protocol, which benchmarked candidate EVA fabrics against samples of Alan Bean's Apollo 12 suit.
  • Microstructural analysis of degraded surfaces. I used SEM, TEM, EBSD, XRD, and optical microscopy to characterize as-deposited coatings and to map the evolution of grain-boundary chemistry and phase distribution after accelerated corrosion and abrasive-wear exposures. EBSD identified which grain-boundary populations preferentially nucleated corrosion-driven microcracks, informing a downstream texture-control intervention on the leading candidate. The mode of failure I was targeting is the one NASA documented on the Apollo 12 ITMG T-164 Teflon outer fabric (see figure below): regolith particles wedged between fibers, prying them apart and fraying individual filaments (Christoffersen et al., NASA TP-2009-214786).
  • Mission-relevant performance outcomes. The resulting coating shortlist delivered measurable improvements over the Apollo-era beta-cloth and Teflon baselines on the metrics that dominate spacesuit and structural-spaceship durability: reduced wear rate under regolith-like abrasion, increased corrosion-resistance margin under simulated atomic-oxygen exposure, and stable hardness across thermal-cycle counts representative of an extended Artemis-class lunar surface mission.
